Before you start

Say the word "provisional" in the first thirty seconds and mean it. The single worst outcome of this meeting is someone leaving with "we lose money on every machine" in their head. The second worst is someone leaving thinking the numbers are final. Frame it as: the dashboard is built, the data underneath it isn't finished yet.

Where we stand

Goal · show the shape, then immediately defuse the margin figure

Here's the shape of the business as the tab currently sees it. 335 customers, 179 of whom have actually bought something. Just under $200,000 of revenue, net of tax. Against that, about $215,000 of variable cost — which gives a contribution margin of negative $15,680.

Pause here

I want to stop on that number, because it's the one everyone will remember, and it's the one I trust least. It says we lose money on every machine we sell. I don't believe that's true, and I'll show you why in a few slides.

Two other things to flag. Acquisition cost reads zero for every single customer — that's not because we don't spend money on ads, it's because the spend can't reach them. And the warranty claim rate is 23.5%, which is measured, real, and probably the most important number on this slide.

If someone jumps in on the margin

Don't defend it — agree and defer. "You're right that it looks bad. Hold that thought for two minutes, I've got a slide on exactly why it's wrong." Then actually get to slide 9.

How to read every number

Goal · teach the four labels so the rest of the deck reads itself

Before the numbers, one thing about how the tab is built, because it changes how you read everything after this.

The model refuses to guess. If we've never priced something, it shows zero and labels it "unpriced" — it doesn't quietly estimate and hope. If a number is a rate multiplied by a quantity, it's marked "estimated". Only figures summed from actual bills are left unmarked.

So: four categories. Measured, estimated, unpriced, and needs fixing. Every figure I show you carries one of them. When you see "unpriced", read the margin as a best case — the real cost is higher than what's on screen.

Worth landing

This is the design decision that makes the whole tab trustworthy even while it's incomplete. It's a good thing to be proud of out loud.

The eleven cost buckets

Goal · orient them on the table, then point at COGS

This is the core of it. Eleven cost buckets, and every dollar of cost belongs to exactly one of them. There's a test that enforces that, so we can't accidentally double-count.

By size: Product COGS at $151,000 is by far the biggest — that's the build cost of the machines. Then refunds at $21,000, shipping at $19,000, warranty at $15,000. Then the smaller ones — return handling, support labour, consumables, 3PL.

Point at the right-hand column

Look at the basis column. Only three of the eleven are fully measured — refunds, consumables, and the invoiced part of shipping. Everything else is estimated, incomplete, or unpriced. That's the honest state of it.

The one I'd have you remember is COGS. It's the biggest bucket by a mile, and it's 79% estimated.

Accurate today

Goal · establish credibility before the bad news

Let's start with the good news, because there is real solid ground here.

Revenue, discounts and tax are exact — straight from order records. Refunds are exact: 18 approvals, all settled, all reconciled. Consumables and parts are exact. Freight is exact wherever we hold an invoice, and we have 169 invoiced charges on file.

The warranty claim rate of 23.5% is measured, and I'd stand behind it. And the US versus Canada split, which I'll come back to, is robust.

Also worth saying: the structure of the model is sound. Eleven buckets, no double counting, covered by tests. The logic isn't what's broken.

2:00

Live, but resting on assumptions

Goal · make COGS the headline of this slide, not the list

Now the things that are live but resting on assumptions. These aren't wrong — the arithmetic is real. But the rate underneath each one was set by hand by one person and never signed off by Finance.

The big one is COGS. Only 72 of our 214 orders use actual build cost from a real manufacturing batch. The other 142 use the roadmap schedule — which is a forecast of what a unit should cost, not what it did cost. In dollar terms that's 79% of our largest cost bucket.

Why that matters: if the schedule is optimistic by ten percent, our headline number moves by about twelve thousand dollars. So this is the single biggest lever on accuracy we have.

Then support labour at $75 an hour. Return handling at $50 plus an hour of inspection. The 3PL rate card. All reasonable numbers — none of them checked against payroll or an actual invoice. And the exchange rate is one fixed number applied across all of history, so older US orders get restated at today's rate.

Real costs priced at zero

Goal · make the ask feel trivially cheap

These three are different. They're fully wired up, and they're showing zero purely because nobody has given us a rate.

Payment processing is the real one. Every order goes through Shopify Payments, Sezzle or QuickBooks, and they all take a cut. On our gross charges, a blended 2.9% would be about $6,700 of cost that's currently invisible.

Sales commission — I genuinely don't know whether we pay it. If we do, it scales with revenue and it matters.

Installation is probably genuinely zero, because we ship self-install. But I'd rather have that confirmed than assumed.

The ask

These are the easiest fixes on the whole list. It's one line of config each — no code, no deploy. The moment Finance gives me two percentages, every margin and LTV figure on the tab updates by itself.

3:00

Known to be wrong

Goal · the whole meeting turns on this slide — slow down

These two I'd call bugs. They're being displayed right now, and they're wrong.

First — the one that explains the headline

We sell machines on monthly payment plans. Every monthly payment arrives in the system as its own separate order — and nothing in the record distinguishes an instalment from a full purchase. So the model charges a complete machine's build cost to every single monthly payment.

That affects 16 customers across 44 orders, and it's put roughly $19,500 of cost on the books that doesn't exist. Correct just that, and the headline margin goes from negative $15,680 to positive $3,800 or thereabouts.

Say this slowly — it's the takeaway

So: we are most likely profitable, and the dashboard is currently saying the opposite because of one costing rule.

It has knock-on effects too. It inflates our order count, so revenue per order looks lower than it really is. And it makes about 39 orders look like they're missing freight when they aren't.

Second — acquisition cost

We have $69,000 of Meta spend sitting in the database. None of it reaches a customer. The sync is pulling lifetime campaign totals instead of a monthly breakdown, so all the spend collapses into a single month back in 2023 — a month in which we acquired nobody. That's why CAC, LTV to CAC and payback are blank across the entire tab.

Both of these are fixable, and neither is a design flaw.

Do not overclaim

The +$3,800 is an estimate, not a restatement. If pressed: it assumes each affected customer should carry one machine's cost, and several of them haven't received a machine at all — so the true correction is probably larger, not smaller. But say "directionally" and "needs Finance to confirm" out loud. Do not let anyone write "+$3,831" on a slide somewhere else.

Data still missing

Goal · brisk. This is a list, not a discussion

Then a set of things that aren't wrong, just incomplete. I'll go quickly.

62 shipped orders have no freight cost on file at all, so freight is understated. Some of that is the instalment issue, some is genuinely uninvoiced.

We have no spend feed for anything except Meta — so 73 customers on organic search, referral, direct and email have no acquisition cost even in principle.

Geography

Goal · deliver the one strong finding, then fence off the weak part

This is the clearest signal on the whole tab, and it holds up despite everything I've just said.

The US book is profitable — 98 customers, plus $33,000, running at almost 27% margin. The Canadian book is not: 163 customers, minus $48,000. Ontario alone is minus $21,000 across 112 customers.

Some of that gap is real. Canadian orders skew heavily to discounted early-bird and pre-order pricing, and essentially all of our warranty replacements have been domestic.

The fence

But don't read too much into the individual province rankings. Manitoba and BC at the bottom there are distorted by the instalment issue — a couple of affected customers in a small province swamps everything else. The country-level split is safe to use. The province-level ranking isn't, yet.

If asked "so should we stop selling in Canada?"

No — and push back on that read. The gap is mostly discounting and warranty concentration, both of which are fixable commercial decisions, not a structural problem with the market. Offer to come back with a clean Canada breakdown once the instalment fix lands.

What we need

Goal · leave with owners and dates for the top four

So here's the ask list, in impact order.

One and two are engineering. We need a flag on orders that identifies an instalment and links it back to the original purchase — that's a Shopify and data-model question. And we need the Meta sync re-run with a monthly breakdown.

Three is Finance, and it's the cheapest thing on the list: a blended payment-processing rate and a commission rate. Two numbers.

Four is manufacturing — actual landed build cost per batch for the remaining 142 orders. That's the biggest accuracy gain available to us.

Five through eight are smaller: a Freightcom invoice export, monthly spend for non-Meta channels, sign-off on the hand-set rates, and one FlexSpace invoice so we can check the 3PL rate card.

Nine is three policy decisions for Finance. Whether refunded HST should net out of the refund figure. Whether we recognise cost when an order is placed or when it ships. And whether we hold one exchange rate or store the rate at the order date.

Ten is data cleanup.

Close the meeting on this

What I'd like out of today is an owner and a rough date for the top four. The rest can follow.

Likely questions

The first three will almost certainly come up.

"So are we profitable or not?"

"On the machines themselves, probably yes, marginally — once the instalment costing is fixed. But I want to be careful: that's contribution margin only. It excludes overheads, salaries, the 3PL's fixed monthly fees, and it excludes acquisition cost entirely because we can't calculate it yet. So 'the unit economics look roughly break-even to slightly positive' is as far as I'd go today."

"How confident are you in the plus $3,800?"

"Directionally confident, precisely not. It assumes each affected customer should carry exactly one machine's cost. Several of them haven't received a machine at all, so the real correction is probably bigger. I'd treat it as 'the sign flips', not as a number."

"Why is COGS estimated? Don't we know what our own machines cost?"

"We do for the batches we've costed — that's the 72 orders. The other 142 were sold before we had batch-level costing in place, or against batches we haven't closed the books on. It's not that the information doesn't exist; it's that it hasn't been entered against those orders. That's ask number four."

"A 23.5% warranty claim rate — is that normal?"

"It's high for hardware, and it's one of the few numbers on the tab I'd fully stand behind. I'd treat it as a genuine finding rather than a data problem. Worth its own session — the tab can tell you which regions and which batches, but not why."

"How long to fix the instalment issue?"

"The engineering is small. The blocker is a decision, not effort: we need to agree how an instalment gets identified — a Shopify tag, a subscription contract ID, or a parent order reference. Once that's decided it's a straightforward change. I'd rather not guess at the mechanism and have to redo it."

"Should we take the tab down until it's right?"

"I'd rather not. Everything on it is labelled with its basis, so it's honest about its own gaps — that's exactly what it was built to do. Taking it down loses the thing that's generating these questions in the first place. What I would do is agree that nobody quotes the margin number externally until we've closed items one through four."

"What is the $69,000 of Meta spend telling us then?"

"Right now, only that we spent it. We can't yet attach it to the customers it won, so we have no cost per acquisition and no LTV-to-CAC ratio anywhere. The good news is the customer side is ready — 198 of our 214 orders already carry a channel. It's purely the spend feed that's broken. Fix that one sync and CAC lights up across the whole tab."

"Why does Canada look so much worse than the US?"

"Three things, and only the third is a data problem. Canadian orders skew heavily to discounted early-bird and pre-order pricing. Essentially all our warranty replacements have been domestic, so that cost concentrates there. And the instalment customers are almost all Canadian, which drags the small provinces badly. The first two are real; the third will partly wash out once we fix it."
